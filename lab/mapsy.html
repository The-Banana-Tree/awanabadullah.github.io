MAPSY

I designed a pool cleaner that did all four jobs instead of one: skim the surface, vacuum the floor, chlorinate, hold the pH. My final-year mechatronic design project at Western University, written up here from the final report.

The problem

Cleaning a pool is four chores, not one, and the session runs about 45 minutes at least twice a week. In 2021 I could buy a machine for any single one of them and nothing at all that did the four together, so full automation meant three or four devices that did not work with each other — and the cleaners among them navigated at random, off bump switches and proximity sensors rather than any idea of where the dirt was. That gap is what I built MAPSY for.

  • SkimFloating leaves and insects off the surface.
  • VacuumSediment off the pool floor.
  • ChlorinateDisinfectant held at a safe concentration.
  • Balance pHAcid or base dosed to keep the water in range.

So I costed mine against that stack. The prototype I actually built came in at about $528 CAD; the production design, machining and electrical safety certification included, at roughly $1,521.

What I was aiming at

I wanted one robot doing all four jobs for less than the stack it replaced: map the pool, steer around obstacles and towards debris, collect it off the surface and off the floor, sink and come back up, dose the chemicals, and keep its own electronics dry throughout. These are the numbers I held it to.

  • Budget$625 CAD for the prototype, $375 of it funded by Western.
  • Runtime1.5 to 3 hours in a 4.5 × 9 × 2.5 m pool, about what the manual job costs you.
  • pHResolution of 0.1, enough to hold the water between 7.2 and 7.8.
  • Chlorine1 to 3 ppm: enough to kill germs, little enough to swim in.
  • Water18 to 40 °C, which covers every season except winter.

Seeing the debris

I wrote out several candidate concepts for each function and scored them on a decision matrix before drawing any of them properly. What I picked: image segmentation from an RGB-D camera for mapping, four ducted propellers for navigation, vacuum suction for collection, active ballasting for submersion rather than fighting buoyancy with downward thrust all day, and a pH meter over a colorimeter.

Mapping is where the real work went. Pool tiles are almost always blue, which turns out to be useful: in an image of a pool floor nearly every pixel has a very high blue-channel value, and anything that does not is probably the thing I am looking for. So I ran the vision on the blue channel alone and split that histogram into foreground and background with Otsu's method, thresholding iteratively until the split settled. Opening and eroding first dropped the isolated specks and smoothed the contours, which matters more underwater than it sounds, because uneven refraction distorts the image constantly. A Kinect gave me the colour image and the depth together, so a segmented piece of debris arrived with its position and its distance for free. The whole segmentation took 0.564 seconds.

My first version had a real flaw: Otsu's method will happily threshold an image into foreground and background whether or not there is any debris in it. It always found something. I fixed it by smoothing the histogram into a continuous line with a kernel density function and then looking at its shape — real debris produced four or more critical points, meaning two genuine peaks, and anything flatter than that meant a clean pool floor and no reason to chase anything.

Moving, sinking, surfacing

Forward, I only had to beat drag. Taking the body as a cylinder — which overestimates the drag coefficient at 1.2, and I wanted the overestimate — with a projected frontal area of 0.296 m² at 0.025 m/s, the drag works out to 0.110 N. A small number, because the robot is slow on purpose. I ran that back through simple momentum theory for a disk actuator, taking the inlet speed as zero for the worst case of starting from rest, and sized the propellers at 53 mm — four of them, each in a duct, on brushless motors, kept straight by a gyro measuring the deviation from a line.

Downward was the harder half. I added up the weight and the displaced volume of every component and got a net buoyancy of +193 N: the robot did not merely float, it floated hard, and nothing about its mass was going to change that. Reaching the floor meant taking on 19.7 litres of water — the volume whose weight cancels that net upward force — and pumping the same water back out to resurface.

My first iteration used a single ballast and it worked, in the sense that the robot sank and resurfaced. It was also a bad idea. One ballast put the buoyant force low and towards the rear, well away from the centre of mass, and a net moment like that on something neutrally buoyant is how a robot ends up upside down on the bottom of a pool. So I went to two, placed so that the moments they apply about the mass centre are equal and opposite and cancel. It is the part I still like best: rather than calculate a centre of buoyancy and then chase it around the frame, the second tank made the calculation unnecessary.

Vacuuming without clogging it

I had two conditions to satisfy with the vacuum: the pressure outside the storage had to exceed the pressure inside it, and the inward flow had to be faster than the robot's own forward speed. Fail the second and the robot simply drives past the debris it is nominally sucking at. I had no prototype budget for the vacuum, so I settled it in SolidWorks Flow Simulation instead — an external study with the motor stepped up in increments until both conditions held, which happened at 250 rad/s and a peak inflow of 1 m/s.

That move was my second refinement. My first layout had the propeller at the back of the storage vessel behind a protective filter, which meant every piece of debris the robot collected went straight onto that filter and stayed there. Moving the propeller to the storage entrance lets debris accumulate behind it and removes the filter entirely — at the cost of needing blades sharp enough that leaves get cut through rather than wrapped around them.

The shell

I hung everything off a PVC pipe network with machined housings — acrylic where something needed to see out, ABS where it needed stiffness, polypropylene for the ballasts — because the chassis failing at depth shorts out every electronic inside it.

So I ran a static FEA study on every subassembly with the water pressure at 3.5 m applied to all surfaces, and the worst factor of safety that came back was greater than 1.6.

What actually got built

This is the part the renders above do not tell you: I never got to assemble the full thing. The project ran through COVID-19, the labs and the public pools were shut, and I could not get the subsystems into one room to put them together. What exists is a set of working subsystems, tested separately, at a scale that fits in a bathroom.

I used a toolbox for the chassis — the right size for every electrical component except the Kinect, which was the most expensive thing in the build, too risky near a possible leak and heavy enough to upset the buoyancy, so I left it out. I drilled holes for the motor shafts, sealed them with washers, and floated the whole thing in a filled bathtub to check for leaks. It did not leak, and it floated.

For the ballast I used an ammunition box, two mini water pumps siliconed into it, and an air-feeding tube to stop it collapsing on itself once emptied. It held 12.5 litres, it was airtight, and in a laundry sink it submerged and resurfaced in 2 minutes.

I prototyped mapping and navigation together, since one drives the other: Kinect, laptop, Arduino, two brushless motors and speed controllers. I put objects in front of the camera on dry land and the motors turned to face the nearest one. The image processing ran on the laptop rather than the Arduino, and by the end MATLAB was driving the motors directly. It proved the loop worked; it did not prove it worked in a pool, and the report said so.

I never built the vacuum or the chemical systems at all, and I dropped both on purpose rather than for lack of time: the chemical loop is a sensor reading a value and a pump dispensing a fixed dose, which is not where the risk was, and the vacuum had been answered well enough in simulation.

Looking back

The honest summary is that I got the subsystems working and never got the robot. Given a room and the time to assemble them, those pieces bolt together.

Two things I would change. The power electronics never delivered the speed the motors were rated for, and the batteries not discharging at the right voltage is the likeliest reason — that wanted measuring properly rather than reasoning about. And segmenting on the blue channel is only as good as the assumption that pools are blue; a small neural network would not care about that, and it is the version I would build now.

MAPSY

I designed a pool cleaner that did all four jobs instead of one: skim the surface, vacuum the floor, chlorinate, hold the pH. My final-year mechatronic design project at Western University, written up here from the final report.

The problem

Cleaning a pool is not one chore, it is four: skim the floating debris, vacuum the sediment off the floor, dose the chlorine, and keep the pH in range. A session takes about 45 minutes and has to happen at least twice a week. Let it slide and you get algae, discoloured water, and the bacteria that cause recreational water illnesses.

You can buy a machine for any one of those jobs. In 2021 there was nothing on the market that did all four. Full automation meant buying three or four devices that did not work together, at roughly $2,670 CAD combined — and the cleaners among them mostly navigated at random, off bump switches and proximity sensors rather than any idea of where the dirt was. There are 10.4 million outdoor pools in the US alone. That gap is what I built MAPSY for.

  • SkimFloating leaves and insects off the surface.
  • VacuumSediment off the pool floor.
  • ChlorinateDisinfectant held at a safe concentration.
  • Balance pHAcid or base dosed to keep the water in range.

What I was aiming at

I wanted one robot doing all four, for less than the stack of single-purpose machines it replaced. It had to map the pool, steer around obstacles and towards debris, collect both floating and sunken debris, sink to the floor and come back up, dose the chemicals, and keep its own electronics dry while doing it.

  • Budget$625 CAD for the prototype, $375 of it funded by Western.
  • Runtime1.5 to 3 hours in a 4.5 × 9 × 2.5 m pool, about what the manual job costs you.
  • pHResolution of 0.1, enough to hold the water between 7.2 and 7.8.
  • Chlorine1 to 3 ppm: enough to kill germs, little enough to swim in.
  • Water18 to 40 °C, which covers every season except winter.

One shape out of a pile of sketches

I wrote out several candidate concepts for each function before drawing any of them properly. Mapping could have been proximity sweeps, SLAM, a ring of fisheye cameras, or plain bump-and-turn. Propulsion could have been waterjets, rudders or ducted fans above the waterline. Debris could have been netted, vortexed or sucked. Sinking could have been constant downward thrust or a ballast. I scored each set on a decision matrix against the objectives.

What I picked: image segmentation from an RGB-D camera for mapping, because it was the most accurate and consistent of the five. Four ducted propellers for navigation, on versatility and power. Vacuum suction for collection, on repeatability. Active ballasting for submersion, because it was far more stable than fighting buoyancy with downward thrust all day. A pH meter over a colorimeter, because it was more accurate, more versatile and cheaper at once. I built the chassis as a network of PVC pipe, so I could rearrange a subsystem without remaking the whole frame.

Seeing the debris

Pool tiles are almost always blue, which turns out to be useful: in an image of a pool floor, nearly every pixel has a very high blue-channel value and anything that does not is probably the thing you are looking for. So I ran the vision on the blue channel alone. A histogram of those values gets split into foreground and background by Otsu's method, thresholding iteratively until the split settles.

Before that, dilation and erosion clean the image up — opening to smooth contours and drop isolated specks, closing to narrow breaks. Underwater that matters more than it sounds, because uneven refraction distorts the image constantly. A Kinect supplied both the colour image and the depth, so once a piece of debris was segmented out, its position and its distance came for free. The whole segmentation took 0.564 seconds, which was fine given how small the Kinect's images are.

My first version had a real flaw: Otsu's method will happily threshold an image into foreground and background whether or not there is any debris in it. It always found something. I fixed it by smoothing the histogram into a continuous line with a kernel density function and then looking at its shape — debris produced four or more critical points, meaning two genuine peaks, and anything flatter than that meant a clean pool floor and no reason to chase anything.

Moving

I only had to beat drag with the propellers. Taking the body as a cylinder — which overestimates the drag coefficient at 1.2, and I wanted the overestimate — with a projected frontal area of 0.296 m² at 0.025 m/s, the drag works out to 0.110 N. That is the thrust the design had to produce, and it is a small number because the robot is slow on purpose.

I ran that back through simple momentum theory for a disk actuator, taking the inlet speed as zero for the worst case of starting from rest, which gave me the propeller area and so the diameter. I sized the propellers at 53 mm, four of them, each in a duct, driven by brushless motors and kept straight by a gyro measuring the deviation from a line.

Sinking, and coming back up

I added up the weight and the displaced volume of every component and got a net buoyancy of +193 N. The robot did not merely float, it floated hard, and nothing about its mass was going to change that. To get it to the floor it had to take on 19.7 litres of water — that is the volume whose weight cancels the net upward force — and pump the same water back out to resurface.

My first iteration used a single ballast and it worked, in the sense that the robot sank and resurfaced. It was also a bad idea. One ballast put the buoyant force low and towards the rear, well away from the centre of mass, and a net moment like that on something neutrally buoyant is how a robot ends up upside down on the bottom of a pool.

So I went to two ballasts, placed so that the moments they apply about the mass centre are equal and opposite and cancel. It is the part I still like best: rather than calculate a centre of buoyancy and then chase it around the frame, the second tank made the calculation unnecessary.

Vacuuming without clogging it

I had two conditions to satisfy with the vacuum: the pressure outside the storage had to exceed the pressure inside it, and the inward flow had to be faster than the robot's own forward speed. Fail the second and the robot simply drives past debris it is nominally sucking at. I had no prototype budget for the vacuum, so I settled it in SolidWorks Flow Simulation instead — an external study with the motor stepped up in increments until both conditions held, which happened at 250 rad/s and a peak inflow of 1 m/s.

That move was my second refinement. My first layout had the propeller at the back of the storage vessel behind a protective filter, which meant every piece of debris the robot collected went straight onto that filter and stayed there. I moved the propeller to the storage entrance instead, which lets debris accumulate behind it and removes the filter entirely — at the cost of needing blades sharp enough that leaves get cut through rather than wrapped around them.

The shell

I hung everything off a PVC pipe network with machined housings: acrylic where something needed to see out or be seen into, ABS where it needed stiffness, polypropylene for the ballasts. The requirement was simple enough — the chassis had to not fail at depth, because the electronics inside it short out if it does.

I ran a static FEA study on every subassembly with the water pressure at 3.5 m applied to all surfaces. The worst factor of safety that came back was greater than 1.6, so the structure was sound with room to spare.

What actually got built

This is the part the renders above do not tell you: I never got to assemble the full thing. The project ran through COVID-19, the labs and the public pools were shut, and I could not get the subsystems into one room to put them together. What exists is a set of working subsystems, tested separately, at a scale that fits in a bathroom.

I used a toolbox for the chassis. It was the right size to hold every electrical component except the Kinect, which was the most expensive thing in the build and too risky to put near a possible leak — it also turned out heavy enough to upset the buoyancy, so I left it out. I drilled holes for the motor shafts, sealed them with washers, and floated the whole thing in a filled bathtub to check for leaks. It did not leak, and it floated.

For the ballast I used an ammunition box, two mini water pumps siliconed into it, and an air-feeding tube to stop it collapsing on itself once emptied — the compressed-air version in the final design would have needed testing I had no way to do. It held 12.5 litres, it was airtight, and in a laundry sink it submerged and resurfaced in 2 minutes.

I prototyped mapping and navigation together, since one drives the other: Kinect, laptop, Arduino, two brushless motors and speed controllers. I put objects in front of the camera on dry land and the motors turned to face the nearest one. The image processing ran on the laptop rather than the Arduino, and by the end MATLAB was driving the motors directly. It proved the loop worked; it did not prove it worked in a pool, and the report said so.

I never built the vacuum or the chemical systems at all. I dropped both on purpose, not for lack of time: the chemical loop is a sensor reading a value and a pump dispensing a fixed dose, which is not where the risk was, and the vacuum had been answered well enough in simulation that I would rather spend the remaining money elsewhere.

What it would have cost

The prototype came in at about $528 CAD against the $625 I had. I costed the production design — mechanical, electrical, machining, and the electrical safety certification a device like this needs — at roughly $1,521 CAD, against the market's four separate machines.

Looking back

The honest summary is that I got the subsystems working and never got the robot. Image segmentation found the nearest debris and gave its position and distance, the motors turned to face it, the ballast sank and resurfaced on a timer, and the vacuum was proven in simulation rather than water. Given a room and the time to assemble them, those pieces bolt together.

Two things I would change. The power electronics never delivered the motor speed the motors were rated for, and the batteries not discharging at the right voltage is the likeliest reason — that wanted measuring properly rather than reasoning about. And segmenting on the blue channel is only as good as the assumption that pools are blue; a pool lit oddly, or debris that happens to sit in the same blue range, defeats it. A small neural network would not care about any of that, and that is the version I would build now.

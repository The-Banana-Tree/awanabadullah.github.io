Hexapod

A 270 mm six-legged robot with every servo on the body and no motor at the knee — the knee is driven through a five-bar linkage instead. First milestone: one leg with all three joints moving, by 15 December 2026.

The goal

Build a walking hexapod of about 270 mm, three degrees of freedom per leg, with all eighteen servos mounted on the body so the moving links stay light. The knee is the interesting part: there is no servo at the joint, so it has to be driven from the hip through a linkage.

The rule I set myself at the start was a basic moving leg before any aesthetic refinement or autonomy work — one leg, three joints, actually moving, before anything gets duplicated six times.

Targets — all provisional, all set to be failed against

  • Speed0.20 m/s on a flat indoor floor, timed over 2 m. 0.40 m/s would be a good day.
  • MassUnder 1.50 kg with battery. Designed against 1.60 kg as a load case.
  • RuntimeFifteen minutes of walking, not fifteen minutes powered on.
  • ObstacleClear a 20 mm threshold.
  • SizeAbout 270 mm, leg tip to leg tip.
  • Load caseAt 1.60 kg on three supporting feet, 5.2 N per foot.

Mechanical architecture

  • Three joints per leg — coxa yaw, femur pitch, knee — and all three servos bolt to the body, so almost no mass is swung out at the end of a leg. On V15 all three are in the assembly, their servos sharing one printed frame.
  • There is no servo at the knee. Both drive inputs sit back on the body and the chain closes through the leg as a five-bar, so the knee angle follows from where those two inputs are rather than from a motor at the joint. This arrived at V12: every revision up to V6 ran a four-bar, and the dated entries below still describe that older arrangement.
  • The tibia is one rigid printed link carrying both of its pivots as separate holes in the same piece. No intermediate hinge.
  • 623ZZ ball bearings, 3 mm bore × 10 mm OD × 4 mm wide, at the hip and the knee — the two joints that carry the bending load. The remaining pivots are plain M3 bolts in reamed holes.
  • Bearing seats capture the outer race in the moving link; printed sleeves touch only the inner race, so tightening the nut clamps the stack without squeezing the joint shut.
  • CoxaYaw about a vertical axis, driven from under the body plate. This is the axis that aims the leg.
  • KneeDriven from the body through the five-bar chain. No servo at the joint.
  • Bearings12 of the 15 ordered go into six legs at two per leg, leaving 3 spare.
  • Plain pivotsPrinted undersize and reamed to a sliding fit on the M3, with a washer between the moving links.

The five-bar schematic has not been drawn yet — the photo at the top of the page is the current reference, and the diagram further down shows the four-bar arrangement it replaced.

Next I need to test the motion limits across the full chain. I will probably have to modify this geometry once I can see where it runs out of travel or binds.

Electronics architecture Designed, not built

Distributed control, chosen 22 October. One Raspberry Pi plans the gait; two ESP32s split the robot by physical side and do their own kinematics. None of it has been wired up yet, and the PWM driver choice is the part I am least sure of.

  • The Pi runs the gait state machine, plans each foot's XYZ waypoints, stamps them with a common start time and duration, and hands them to both boards. It does not stream individual servo increments.
  • Each ESP32 takes three legs — nine channels — and is nobody's master. It validates the command, aligns to the shared start time, interpolates, runs inverse kinematics, clamps geometry and speed limits, and reports completion or fault. One board can run stored trajectories on the bench without the Pi.
  • The left/right split is about keeping wiring short and the harness mirrored. The tripod gait is a software grouping and cuts across both boards.
  • A PCA9685 per side generates the servo PWM, because a stock ESP32 does not have nine good PWM outputs to spare. This is the least settled box on the diagram.
  • The inverse kinematics has to model the five-bar chain, mapping the two body-side input angles to the foot. Treating the tibia as a directly driven servo joint would be wrong, and is the specific mistake this mechanism invites.
  • Hobby RC servos report nothing back. Forward kinematics gives an expected foot position, never a measured one, and labelling an open-loop estimate as actual position is a trap I want to stay out of.
  • Servos run from a separate regulated rail sharing ground with the logic — never off a 3.3 V board pin. Leg 1 on the bench needs 6 V at 7.5 A peak, which is three MG996Rs stalling at once.

Decisions and iterations

  1. 10 Nov 2026

    V15, with all three joints in one frame. Three revisions on from V12 and the leg now carries every joint it is supposed to have — the coxa plus the two body-side inputs that close the five-bar — with all three servos stacked into a single printed frame instead of the bracket V12 used. That closes the coxa question I left open yesterday: the axis is in the assembly, not just the CAD.

    Next I need to test the motion limits across the full chain, and I expect to modify this geometry once I can see where it runs out of travel or binds.

  2. 9 Nov 2026

    V12, and the knee is a five-bar now. I moved off the four-bar that every revision up to V6 used — V12 closes the chain as a five-bar instead. It is also the first leg that is printed, assembled and carrying its servos on a printed bracket, with more bulk taken out of the links again, and I can pick the whole thing up by one end with no glue in it, which I could not do with the leg that cracked in October.

    Still open: the coxa axis, and whether I can package eighteen MG996R cases inside a body that is meant to come in under 1.50 kg.

  3. 2 Nov 2026

    First servo in hand, and in CAD. The MG996R arrived and is now modelled in Onshape with its horn arm, so the mounts get drawn around the real shape instead of a datasheet drawing. It stays the tentative leg 1 servo until the arrival checks are done — weight, usable travel, pulse range per unit, horn spline fit on the printed hubs, deadband jitter at rest.

  4. 28 Oct 2026

    Swapped to a servo I know is too heavy. I bought three Miuzei MG996Rs for leg 1 instead of the 17 g AGFRC B13DLM V2 I had been considering. The MG996R gives 11 kgf·cm stalled at 6 V against the B13DLM's 3.8, and at an illustrative 100 mm foot reach the 5.2 N static load needs about 5.3 kgf·cm at the femur — so the first powered test has roughly a factor of two in hand instead of none.

    The cost is weight, and it is not a small one. At 55 g each, eighteen come to about 990 g against a 378 g servo budget, putting the whole robot at roughly 1.56–1.98 kg — over the 1.50 kg target. The bigger case also makes hiding servos inside the body harder. I took it for leg 1 only; the eighteen-servo choice stays open until one leg has been loaded.

  5. 23 Oct 2026

    V6 went together without breaking. The reprinted leg is visibly slimmer than the one that cracked and assembled clean — the first build that did. The crank still hits its own fasteners before the end of its travel, so that is the open fault, and the hold point is explicit: resolve the interference and show smooth unpowered motion before any servo mount is finalised or any load is applied.

  6. 22 Oct 2026

    Split the control problem by side. One Pi for gait, two ESP32s for nine servos each, divided left and right. The reason is wiring length and a mirrored harness, not the gait itself — the tripod grouping cuts across both boards and lives in software. It also means one board and one leg is a complete bench setup, which is what I actually need next.

  7. 21 Oct 2026

    The femur broke while I was assembling it. The knee half went together fine — crank, pushrod and tibia bolted up with the bearing seated. Adding the femur to make the first whole leg split it during assembly, and several of the M3 lengths I had picked turned out wrong for their joint stacks. Hot glue is holding it for hand-set poses only; nothing gets driven or loaded on a glued leg.

    What I took from it: measure the whole grip stack — link thicknesses, bearing, sleeves, washers, nut — before choosing a bolt length, get full nyloc engagement without using the nut to crush a free pivot, check clearance and free rotation at all three moving joints before final tightening, and reinforce the fractured section around the pivot bosses while reviewing print orientation.
